A few hundred kilometres can make a remarkable difference in the automotive business.

Hungary, Slovakia and Czechia are neighbours. They share borders, are part of the same economic region and, at first glance, their automotive markets may seem quite similar.

The numbers tell a more nuanced story.

According to ACEA data, the average passenger car on European roads is around 12.5 years old. But in Czechia, the average is 16.2 years. In Slovakia, 15.1 years. In Hungary, 15.0 years.

And vehicle age is only one variable.

The pace of electrification, purchasing habits, fleet structures, taxation, infrastructure, dealer networks and even the expectations customers have towards a brand can differ significantly from one country to another.

It is tempting to look at Central Europe as one automotive market.

Geographically, that makes sense. Commercially, it can be misleading.

A strategy that works in one country cannot simply be copied, translated and launched in the next. The brand may remain the same, but the market around it changes.

This is something we experience every day at Grand Automotive Central Europe.

Operating across multiple markets gives us the opportunity to see both sides: the similarities that create regional scale, and the local differences that determine whether a strategy actually works. Central Europe is one region. But it is not one automotive market. And perhaps that is exactly what makes working in this region so interesting.

Buying a vehicle today means entering an ecosystem that will stay with you for years. Think about what happens after someone drives away from the dealership. They may use an app to check the vehicle remotely or plan a journey. Software updates can add functions or improve existing ones without a workshop visit. An EV owner becomes familiar with charging networks and charging apps. Financing, insurance, roadside assistance and digital services continue running quietly in the background.

A growing part of the ownership experience now happens outside the vehicle itself.

This has developed remarkably quickly. For a long time, the relationship was relatively straightforward. A customer bought a car, returned to the dealership for servicing and repairs, and eventually came back when it was time for another one. Most of the experience was built around those physical interactions.

Today, manufacturers are developing vehicles and digital platforms alongside each other. Dealers are introducing customers to connected services as part of the handover. Technology companies, charging providers, financial institutions and other partners have all become part of a network that needs to work together throughout the vehicle's lifetime.

This also changes what automotive distribution looks like.

At Grand Automotive Central Europe, bringing a vehicle to market is still a fundamental part of what we do. But getting the product into the showroom is only one part of the work behind a modern automotive brand.

New technologies have to be introduced across the dealer network. Teams need the knowledge to explain connected features to customers. Digital services have to work in individual markets. Charging, financing and other partnerships need to be established locally. And when a manufacturer introduces something new, that experience ultimately has to make sense for the person sitting in the driver's seat.

Customers rarely think about how many companies and systems are involved in making all of this work. They shouldn't need to. They simply experience whether their car, app, dealership and services feel like parts of the same ownership journey.

That expectation is gradually redefining what it means to bring a vehicle to market.

What does washing a car have to do with vehicle quality? Quite a lot, apparently. When Nissan evaluates the paintwork on a vehicle, its inspectors consider how an owner might look at the body while washing it. From that close distance, scratches, colour irregularities and other details become much easier to notice. It is one small example of a much larger approach to quality.

Nissan uses its Vehicle Evaluation Standard, or VES, to assess vehicles against more than 300 criteria developed from the customer's perspective. The same criteria are used at Nissan production sites around the world.

Some aspects of vehicle quality can be measured relatively easily. Others become meaningful when you think about how somebody will actually live with the finished car. What will they notice when they see it from a few centimetres away? How will something feel after hundreds of uses? What happens when the vehicle is driven in the situations its owner will encounter every day?

Nissan applies the same thinking to dynamic evaluations. Test vehicles are driven in different imagined customer-use scenarios, with evaluators assessing how they drive, corner and stop, as well as the performance of technologies such as driver assistance systems. Evaluations also continue periodically after mass production has begun.

This customer perspective is also what makes Nissan's approach to quality relevant to our work at Grand Automotive Central Europe.

As an importer, we operate where the standards established by the manufacturer eventually meet customers in individual markets. By the time a Nissan reaches us, hundreds of decisions about its engineering, materials, manufacturing and quality have already been made. Together with the dealer network, we then see how those decisions translate into everyday ownership: what customers notice, which details matter to them and what questions emerge once the cars are being used every day.

Nissan's 300+ criteria are built around much the same idea. Quality ultimately has to make sense from the perspective of the person experiencing the vehicle, and that perspective continues to change. Nissan therefore reviews its targets and criteria as customer expectations evolve, with feedback contributing to the further development of its evaluation system.

The distance between an engineering standard and a customer's everyday experience can sometimes be surprisingly small. Sometimes, it is simply the distance between someone's eyes and the paintwork while they wash their car.

A model sold in more than 70 countries is about to add Europe to the map. The Nissan Kicks has already surpassed 1.8 million sales globally, with the second-generation model currently produced in Japan, Mexico and Brazil. Now, for the first time, the compact crossover is heading to European customers.

And its European debut comes with 2 important developments. The Kicks will arrive with Nissan's third-generation e-POWER hybrid technology and will be manufactured at Nissan's Sunderland plant in the UK following a £170 million investment.

e-POWER takes a distinctive approach to hybrid driving. The wheels are driven exclusively by an electric motor, while the petrol engine generates electricity and recharges the onboard battery. This gives drivers the smooth acceleration and immediate response of electric drive while retaining the familiarity of refuelling at a petrol station.

More than 1.9 million e-POWER vehicles have been sold globally since the technology launched in 2016, including 200,000 Qashqai e-POWER models produced in Sunderland.

For Grand Automotive Central Europe, the arrival of Kicks adds another option to an increasingly diverse Nissan electrified portfolio in Europe.

Customers across our markets have different driving patterns, access to charging and expectations from their vehicles. A broader mix of electrified powertrains gives them more ways to find a technology that works for their everyday use.

There is also a significant European manufacturing story behind the announcement. Sunderland celebrates 40 years of Nissan production this year and has now built 12 million vehicles. Kicks will become the 10th model allocated to the plant since the first Nissan Bluebird left the production line in 1986. It will be built alongside Qashqai, Juke and LEAF, with the next-generation 100% electric JUKE also due to enter production there from next year.

A model already familiar to customers around the world is therefore entering Europe with something distinctly European behind it too: its production.

That matters as electric vehicles reach a much broader group of customers.

The series covers the fundamentals of how an EV works, batteries, real-world range and efficiency, charging and the ways energy stored in a vehicle could also be used beyond driving.

The individual topics are useful, but for us at Grand Automotive Central Europe, the thinking behind the series is equally important.

The automotive industry has invested enormously in developing electric vehicles. Batteries have improved, ranges have increased, charging has become faster and the choice of models continues to grow. But technological progress alone does not automatically make a new technology feel familiar.

Customers need information they can actually use.

This is especially relevant when a purchase changes established habits. Filling a fuel tank is something most drivers have understood for decades. The automotive industry isn't just changing the cars we drive. It's changing the people who work with them. Over the past decade, vehicles have become more connected, more electrified and more software-driven than ever before. That transformation has reshaped far more than the products themselves. It has changed the skills the industry depends on every day.

A technician today is expected to understand advanced diagnostics, high-voltage systems and increasingly sophisticated electronics alongside traditional mechanical expertise. Sales professionals are helping customers navigate topics like charging, battery technologies and connected services. Trainers have to keep dealership networks up to date in an environment where technology evolves faster than ever, while service teams continue learning as new systems and software are introduced.

In many ways, the automotive industry is facing two transformations at once. One is technological. The other is human.

Every new innovation creates a demand for new knowledge. Every new vehicle platform requires people who know how to explain it, maintain it and support it throughout its lifetime. As a result, continuous learning is no longer an advantage. It's becoming an essential part of working in automotive.
That also raises an important question for the future.

Who will become the next generation of technicians, trainers, service specialists and automotive professionals? As the industry continues to evolve, attracting, developing and retaining skilled people is becoming just as important as developing the next generation of vehicles.

At Grand Automotive Central Europe, we see investing in people as an investment in the entire customer journey. The strength of a dealer network depends not only on the vehicles it represents, but also on the expertise, confidence and commitment of the people who work with those vehicles every day.

The automotive industry has always been driven by innovation. Today, innovation depends just as much on the people who are willing to keep learning as it does on the technology itself.

Some sustainability decisions begin with something as small as choosing what a single car part should be made from. David Lipka has been thinking about those decisions since joining Nissan as a materials engineer in 2011. Today, as Senior Manager of Materials Engineering in the US, his work sits at the intersection of quality, cost and environmental impact.

His perspective is simple: every vehicle and every part requires materials, so choosing and managing those materials well creates an opportunity to address several challenges at once.

We can see that thinking in one of David's early projects involving the 2013 Nissan LEAF produced in Smyrna, Tennessee. The team was looking at plastic waste, CO₂ emissions and the cost of electric vehicles together, exploring how decisions at material level could contribute to each.

It is a useful reminder of how broad automotive sustainability has become.

Electrification naturally receives much of the attention, but the environmental footprint of a vehicle is shaped by decisions throughout its lifecycle. The materials used in thousands of components matter. So does the energy required to produce them, how efficiently resources are used, whether waste can be reduced and what happens to those materials later.

At the scale of global vehicle production, improvements that appear small at component level can become significant when repeated across thousands or millions of cars.

There is a business dimension to this as well. During the material shortages caused by COVID-19, David's team had to validate alternatives quickly while maintaining Nissan's quality standards. It showed how material choices connect sustainability with cost, quality and supply-chain resilience.
At Grand Automotive Central Europe, we encounter these decisions at the other end of the process.

By the time a vehicle reaches one of our markets, years of engineering and thousands of choices have already shaped the finished product. As an importer, sustainability therefore means understanding the thinking behind the vehicles we bring to market as well as considering our own role in their journey from manufacturer to customer.

The transition towards more sustainable mobility is built through countless decisions across the entire automotive value chain. Some are highly visible. Others may be hidden inside a component most drivers will never think about. Their combined impact is what matters.

Some of the most useful information about a vehicle only becomes available after it has left the showroom. Once cars are being driven every day, customers start noticing things that are difficult to capture during development. They use features differently, ask questions nobody expected to come up with and develop their own opinions about everything from technology and comfort to servicing and digital functions.

For an importer, this creates an interesting flow of information.

We sit between the manufacturer, the dealer network and the markets where the vehicles are actually being used. Dealers speak to customers every day, which means the network gradually builds a very detailed picture of how people experience a brand locally. The challenge is making sure those observations do not stop at the dealership.

This is something Nissan has built into its approach to quality.

The company collects customer comments and questions around the world through dealerships, contact centres and surveys. That feedback is shared across functions including product planning, design, R&D, manufacturing, sales and service, giving different parts of the organisation access to what customers are actually saying about their vehicles.

What we find particularly relevant at Grand Automotive Central Europe is Nissan's definition of quality itself. Mechanical reliability is part of it, but the company also looks at other factors that can leave a customer dissatisfied. That distinction matters when operating across different countries.

Expectations are rarely identical from one market to another. Climate and road conditions differ. So do driving habits, infrastructure and the way customers use their vehicles. Nissan develops its vehicles around global specifications while also recognising that certain elements need to be fine-tuned for regional needs. This is where local feedback becomes especially valuable.

A single comment from one customer may tell us very little. When dealerships across a market begin hearing the same questions or observing similar behaviour, a pattern starts to emerge. When those insights can then travel through the importer and ultimately reach the manufacturer, local experience becomes useful information for a global organisation.

Nissan supports this culture internally as well. Customer Centric Workshops encourage employees to look at their work from the customer's perspective, while Nissan Quality Forums bring together employees and suppliers around quality results, customer feedback and areas for improvement.

For us, that completes an important circle. Vehicles and technologies travel from manufacturers into individual markets. What customers learn from living with them should have a route back.

When people think about buying a car, they usually picture the moment they drive it home. The vehicle takes centre stage, while everything that happens behind the scenes often goes unnoticed. Yet much of the customer experience is shaped long before delivery, and continues long after it.

Before a new model reaches a showroom, a complex network of logistics, inventory planning and dealer preparation is already in motion. Vehicles need to arrive on time, parts must be available, technicians require specialised training, and retailers need the knowledge and tools to support customers from day one.

The journey doesn't end after delivery either. Warranty support, parts availability, software updates and responsive customer service have become essential parts of vehicle ownership. As cars become increasingly connected and technologically advanced, maintaining a positive ownership experience extends far beyond traditional servicing.

For customers, these processes are often invisible, and that's exactly the point. When everything works as it should, appointments are straightforward, parts arrive when needed, updates are seamless and support is readily available. It's only when one part of that system breaks down that people realise how much happens behind the scenes.

For automotive importers and distributors, delivering a great customer experience means looking far beyond the vehicle itself. It requires close collaboration with manufacturers, logistics partners and dealer networks to ensure that every stage of the ownership journey is supported.

At Grand Automotive Central Europe, we believe that great customer experiences are built through the countless processes customers rarely see. Because while a vehicle may create the first impression, it's the support behind it that builds long-term confidence.

The best customer experience isn't created in a single moment. It's built every day, long after the keys have changed hands.
